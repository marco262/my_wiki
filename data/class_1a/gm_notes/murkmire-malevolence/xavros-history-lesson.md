[[breadcrumb /class_1a/gm_notes/murkmire-malevolence/The Murkmire Malevolence|The Murkmire Malevolence]]

## Purpose

Use this as an optional cold open before the museum trip. It gives the players a short in-character classroom scene, introduces the adventure's theme of acting responsibly under uncertainty, and makes the field trip feel like a natural follow-up to schoolwork rather than a random excursion.

## Framing

**Teacher:** [Professor Xavros](^class_1a/characters/xavros.png)  
**Class Type:** History, with a practical adventuring focus  
**Tone:** Calm, serious, and just a little theatrical  
**Suggested Length:** 5–10 minutes of table time  
**Best Use:** The day before the students depart for the museum

## Teaching Goal

Xavros wants the students to absorb one lesson:

**Adventurers almost never get complete information. Your job is not to wait for certainty. Your job is to notice what matters, judge risk honestly, and choose the least harmful path you can defend.**

## Delivery Notes

Play Xavros as patient, exacting, and quietly grand. He is not trying to scare the students, but he is trying to teach them that history is full of people who made poor decisions because they mistook confidence for understanding.

Good table rhythm:

1. Xavros sets up the old story.
2. He pauses at key points and asks the students what they think.
3. Let the players answer briefly in character.
4. Xavros affirms part of the answer, then sharpens it.
5. He ends with a practical principle, not a sermon.

## Opening Description

The history lecture hall smells faintly of old paper, lamp oil, and polished wood. Professor Xavros stands at the front of the room with his hands folded behind his back, long and still as a statue until he chooses to move. When he does move, it is with the deliberate ease of someone who never hurries because he expects the room to keep pace with him.

He lets the class settle, then says:

> "Today we will discuss a famous failure in the history of adventuring."
>
> "Not a failure of courage. Not even a failure of skill."
>
> "A failure of judgment."

He turns and writes three words on the board:

> **Evidence. Risk. Duty.**

Then he faces the class again.

> "You will discover, if you live long enough, that most disasters begin with someone being too certain about what they know."

## The Story

> "Three hundred and twelve years ago, an adventuring company called the Company of the Hollow Dawn crossed the ash valleys east of ruined Tel Avaris."
>
> "They were competent. Experienced. Famous, in their day. Their company leader was Sir Caldrin Vale, a knight-errant of some repute. With him traveled the mage Irieth Sableglass, the scout Toma Fen, the dwarf antiquarian Brunna Coalvein, and a young temple warden named Sister Halden."
>
> "In a collapsed observatory beneath the ruins, they found an object now called the Black Reliquary Mask of Tel Avaris."

Pause, then continue:

> "Despite the name, it was not a mask anyone could wear."
>
> "It was a face-sized plate of black glass or polished stone, shaped in the likeness of a calm human face with closed eyes and no mouth. Silver filaments ran through it like trapped lightning. It was cool to the touch, but frost gathered on the pedestal beneath it. When moonlight struck it, the silver lines brightened and nearby whispers seemed to echo twice. Animals refused to go near it. The dead ivy in the ruin had begun whitening and going brittle around its stand."

### Question 1

Xavros looks over the room.

> "Very well. If you were first to examine this relic, how would you go about documenting it?"

**Good student answers to reward:**

* what it looks like
* where it was found
* what happens around it
* whether it is magical
* whether animals, plants, or people react strangely to it

**Xavros response:**

> "Good. Description before conclusion."
>
> "Too many adventurers write, 'we found a relic of power,' when what they mean is, 'we found something strange and began guessing.'"

## What the Company Thought

> "Brunna Coalvein believed it was a funerary seal placed over the dead."
>
> "Irieth Sableglass believed it was a magical focusing device or ward-key."
>
> "Sister Halden believed it was sealed for a reason and should not be moved until they understood more."
>
> "Sir Caldrin believed delay was its own danger. Tel Avaris was unstable, rival treasure-seekers were nearby, and if the object mattered, leaving it in the ruin might be the greater mistake."
>
> "All of them had evidence. None of them had enough."

### Question 2

> "Which of them sounds most right to you so far?"

Let the students argue a little.

**Xavros response after a few answers:**

> "An irritating answer, perhaps, but the truth is that several of them were being sensible."
>
> "This is the point. In the field, your choices are often not between right and wrong."
>
> "They are between several incomplete judgments."

## The First Mistake

> "The company chose to move the object."
>
> "That was not, by itself, the mistake."
>
> "The mistake was that they treated movement as proof of understanding."
>
> "They wrapped it in ward-cloth, placed it in a padded chest, and brought it to a frontier keep called Rosemarch, where they intended to examine it in safety."
>
> "On the journey, the horses grew agitated. One porter swore that when the chest was opened for inspection at dusk, the face on the mask no longer looked calm, but strained. Sister Halden punished him for drunkenness."

### Question 3

> "At that moment, what would you do?"

Keep the question concrete for the kids. If they stall, offer choices:

* stop and inspect it again
* keep moving but increase precautions
* hand it to a temple or mage
* bury or abandon it

**Xavros response:**

> "Yes. Reassess."
>
> "The world does not owe you consistency. If new evidence appears, your old plan may no longer deserve your loyalty."

## The Second Mistake

> "At Rosemarch, the local lord faced a problem with no tidy answer."
>
> "He did not want a dangerous unknown hidden in some cellar where rumors could grow worse than truth. He wanted the object examined before witnesses, catalogued by recognized experts, and kept under his protection inside the keep until it was fully understood."
>
> "There was some sense in this. A sealed inspection meant guards, scribes, priests, and mages would all be present. If the object proved valuable, cursed, or politically important, there would be a public record of how it was handled."
>
> "But Brunna objected. Sister Halden objected. Irieth wanted one more day to study it away from a crowd."
>
> "The lord believed delay would breed fear, theft, and argument over custody. He also, it must be said, preferred a solution that kept authority visibly in his own hands."
>
> "So he chose the most orderly option."
>
> "During the examination, the silver filaments in the mask flared all at once."

Pause.

> "The face did not crack like stone."
>
> "It folded inward like a door unlatching."

Let that sit.

> "Inside was not treasure, nor a spirit bound in a gem, nor sacred ash. It was a prison-lock, and something had been held behind it. Contemporary accounts disagree on whether what emerged was beast, spirit, or something between. What matters is that they broke a seal they did not understand, in the middle of a fortified town, because important adults preferred a public explanation to a cautious one."

### Question 4

> "Who made the biggest mistake?"

This is a good point for short player discussion.

**Useful angles to validate:**

* the lord, for pride
* Caldrin, for ignoring warning signs
* the whole company, for moving it too confidently
* no one person, because the real problem was uncertainty handled badly

**Xavros response:**

> "Also irritating, but again: more than one answer is defendable."
>
> "Pride worsened the danger. Dismissal blinded them. Hurry narrowed their thinking."
>
> "No single foolish act doomed them. A chain of reasonable acts, poorly reexamined, did."

## The Resolution

> "The creature broke free, injured seven people, and nearly escaped the keep."
>
> "Sister Halden and Brunna forced it back into the observatory chest while Irieth reworked the wards by hand. Sir Caldrin held the breach long enough for them to seal it, and took wounds that forced his retirement from adventuring."
>
> "In the end, the object was not destroyed. It was returned to the ruins under guard, the chamber collapsed, and the site marked forbidden."
>
> "Later historians argued for decades about whether that was the correct choice."

### Question 5

> "So then. What is the lesson?"

Let the students answer in simple terms.

Good answers to pull out:

* write down facts before guessing
* pay attention when circumstances change
* if something might be dangerous, be careful before being proud
* disagreeing experts do not mean no one knows anything
* you may have to act without certainty

## Xavros's Conclusion

Xavros nods once or twice as the students answer, then sums it up:

> "History is not a neat ledger of obvious mistakes."
>
> "Most people who caused calamity believed they were being practical."
>
> "So, when you encounter something old, powerful, half-understood, or badly labeled, ask three questions."

He taps the board.

> "What do we know?"
>
> "What are we only assuming?"
>
> "Who is endangered if we are wrong?"

Then, after a beat:

> "That last question is the most important."
>
> "Men are often tempted to protect reputation, property, or pride first."
>
> "A good adventurer learns to notice the difference."

## Transition to the Field Trip

You can end the scene with a clean segue:

> "Fortunately for all of you," Professor Xavros says, with the faintest suggestion of dry amusement, "Miss Elleewick's field excursion tomorrow is not merely to any museum collection."
>
> "You are going specifically to see the Murkmire Stone, a recently unearthed object from the marsh ruins south of Varkenbluff. It does not match any well-established local tradition, its material has not been confidently identified, and its purpose remains disputed. Which, naturally, makes it important."
>
> "The museum believes it is safe to display. The archaeologists who recovered it clearly believed it was safe to move. Good. Ask yourselves why."
>
> "Ask what evidence convinced them. Ask what dangers they ruled out. Ask what assumptions they are treating as settled fact."
>
> "Observe what is labeled. Observe what is emphasized. Observe what no one around you seems interested in questioning."
>
> "History is most dangerous when people mistake a confident story for the truth."

At that, the lesson ends and the class is sent to gather for the trip.

## Optional Player-Facing Prompt

If you want one final quick roleplay beat before leaving the classroom, ask each player:

* What part of the story stuck with your character most?
* Which person in the story do they think was most right?
* What would they have done differently?

Keep answers brief. The point is to get the students thinking like young adventurers before the museum begins.

## Why This Works for Murkmire

This cold open quietly prepares the players for:

* a prestigious institution being wrong about an object
* incomplete and disputed information
* the need to update a plan when new danger signs appear
* the idea that containment can matter more than ownership
* student judgment mattering even when adults are present
* a field trip that is explicitly about examining how professionals interpreted the Murkmire Stone
